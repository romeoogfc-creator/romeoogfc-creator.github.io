Tiến Lên strategy: how to win more hands

Luck decides the cards you get. Skill decides what you do with them. These are the habits that separate strong Tiến Lên players from everyone else, roughly in the order most people learn them.

1. Plan the whole hand before your first play

When your cards arrive, sort them and count how many separate plays you would need to get rid of everything. A hand that can be played out in five plays is much stronger than one that needs nine, even if the second hand has more high cards. Look for straights and pairs that fit together without leaving lonely low cards behind. Strong players often play a hand in their head first: "my straight from 4 to 8, my pair of jacks, my three kings, then my 2 and my last single."

The game shows you a sorted hand and a SORT button; use it to look at your cards both by rank and grouped into combinations.

2. Get rid of your weakest cards early

Low singles, such as a lonely 4 or 5, are the cards most likely to be left in your hand at the end, because almost anything beats them. Whenever you start a round, think about which weak card or combination you can unload. Starting a round with your best combination wastes control; starting with your worst gets it out of the way while you still have strong cards to win the turn back.

3. Do not break good combinations without a reason

Splitting a straight to beat a single, or breaking a pair to stop someone, often leaves you with two or three extra weak cards. Sometimes it is right, for example to stop a player who is about to go out, but it should be a deliberate choice, not a habit. Ask yourself how many plays your hand will need after the move.

4. Treat your 2s as tools, not trophies

A 2 wins almost any single round, so it is your way of taking control back. Use it at a moment when winning the round lets you start a new round with something useful, for example a long straight nobody can beat. Holding a 2 until the end is risky: if you finish last you pay extra for it (thối), and a 2 played too late may be chopped. Many experienced players keep one 2 as a final key to close out the hand.

Remember that a red 2 costs twice as much as a black 2 when it is chopped. If you have both, it is often wiser to play the black 2 first when you suspect someone holds a bomb.

5. Use bombs at the right moment

Three consecutive pairs, four of a kind and four consecutive pairs are your bombs. Their value is highest when they chop a 2, especially a red 2 or a pair of 2s, because the chop wins points and takes control in one move. Using a bomb as an ordinary combination early in the hand is usually a waste. On the other hand, do not wait so long that the hand ends with the bomb still in your hand, because then it counts against you in thối.

Keep in mind which bombs can chop which plays: three pairs only chop a single 2, four of a kind cannot chop a pair of 2s, and only four consecutive pairs can be played out of turn.

6. Count the 2s and the bombs

There are only four 2s in the deck. Keep track of how many have been played. When three are gone and you hold the fourth, your 2 is the highest single left in the game, and you can plan around it. In the same way, once all four cards of a rank have been seen, no one can have four of a kind of that rank. The Hard computer players in Tiến Lên Nova count cards like this; you can too.

7. Watch how many cards each player has left

The number of cards in each opponent's hand is shown next to their name. When someone is down to one or two cards, think carefully before starting a round with a single or a pair: you may be giving them exactly what they need to go out. Start with a straight or a triple instead, or play a high single that they are unlikely to beat.

8. Never risk cóng

If another player goes out before you have played any card, you are cóng and pay a heavy penalty. Early in the hand, if you get a chance to play something without hurting your plan, take it. Passing every round while saving a perfect hand can end in disaster if an opponent has a fast hand.

9. Think about who you let win the round

Passing is a choice. Sometimes it is better to let a player who is far from going out win a round than to spend a strong card on it. Save your strength for the moments when the next round matters: when you can start with your best combination, or when you need to stop a player who is close to winning.

10. Practise against harder opponents

The fastest way to improve is to play many hands and notice what went wrong when you lose. Start with the Easy computer players, move to Normal, and when you win often, try Hard. Then invite friends or family to an online table: people are less predictable than computers.
